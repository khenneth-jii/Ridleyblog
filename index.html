Will SOC Analysts Be Replaced by AI?

Exploring the future of cybersecurity, autonomous security operations, and why human judgment still matters.

A Discussion on Agentic AI and the Future of SOC

Introduction

As AI is becoming more common in everyday life, including education, businesses, healthcare and communication, it is also being introduced into cybersecurity. Many businesses are beginning to use AI to help protect their systems against cyber threats.

Within a Security Operations Centre (SOC), it can be difficult to identify real threats when there are hundreds of alerts to check. Because of this, serious threats might not receive attention quickly enough, especially when analysts have many other tasks to complete.

In his 2025 IBM article, How agentic AI enables an autonomous SOC with minimal human involvement, John Velisaris explains how AI could help SOC teams become more efficient. He compares the future SOC to a modern aeroplane. Many processes can happen automatically, but a pilot is still needed to monitor the situation and take control when something unexpected happens.

In the same way, AI could handle regular security tasks while human analysts become the "SOC pilots" who step in when necessary (Velisaris, 2025).

I believe this could be useful because it would allow security teams to work more efficiently and respond to threats faster. However, I also have concerns about leaving important security decisions to AI. If AI makes an incorrect decision or receives inaccurate information, it could create problems for the business. Companies need to think carefully about how much control they give to AI.

Tasks AI Can Complete

I believe AI can be very useful for completing repetitive tasks. Within a SOC, there may be hundreds of alerts to process. As a result, security analysts may not have enough time to manually check every alert.

By using agentic AI, many of these tasks can be automated. AI agents can collect information, detect suspicious activities and decide which actions may be appropriate.

For example, imagine an employee logging into their account from a different location than usual. An AI agent could check previous login activities, identify the device being used and compare the activity with the employee's normal behaviour.

If something suspicious is found, the AI can prepare a report and send it to the security team. This allows the analyst to understand what happened more quickly.

Examples of tasks AI can complete:

  • Identifying unusual activity.
  • Grouping similar security alerts together.
  • Detecting common malware and suspicious logins.
  • Creating reports about possible security problems.
  • Identifying missing security updates.
  • Responding to known threats using pre-approved actions.
My view: AI is most useful when handling tasks that are predictable and have clear rules. However, businesses should still decide which actions can be automated and which ones need human approval.

For example, blocking a suspicious login is different from turning off an important company server. If AI disconnects a server that supports important business activities, it could affect many people. This is why some decisions should still require human validation.

Why Human Analysts Are Still Required

It might seem that because AI can detect threats quickly, human analysts may no longer be needed. However, I do not completely agree with this idea.

Cyber threats can be unpredictable. Hackers are constantly finding new ways to attack systems, making some threats difficult to identify. One example mentioned by Velisaris (2025) is a zero-day attack. These attacks take advantage of vulnerabilities that have not yet been identified or properly addressed.

This is where human experience becomes important. AI may not have enough information to understand a new attack. Human analysts can investigate the situation, consider other possible explanations and decide what action should be taken.

For example, an AI agent might detect unusual activity on a company server and recommend disconnecting it. However, if that server handles customer information or important business activities, disconnecting it immediately could cause serious problems.

Decisions that should involve human analysts:

  • When a new type of attack is detected.
  • When AI is uncertain about what is happening.
  • When an action could affect important business systems.
  • When there is missing or incomplete information.
  • When customer information or privacy could be affected.
  • When the possible damage is too serious for an automatic decision.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) explains that AI systems should be reliable, safe, secure and accountable. Its AI Risk Management Framework encourages organisations to understand and manage the risks connected to using AI (Tabassi, 2023).

I think this is important because AI should not be trusted simply because it can make decisions faster than humans. Analysts should still be able to review its decisions and take control when needed.

Incorrect or Missing Information on Assets

One important point in the IBM article is the need for accurate information about company assets. To make an appropriate decision, AI needs to understand which device is affected and what that device is used for (Velisaris, 2025).

For example, an ordinary employee computer and a critical company server should not always receive the same response. If malware infects an employee's computer, disconnecting it from the network may be a sensible action. However, disconnecting an important server could interrupt business operations.

This shows why context is so important in cybersecurity. AI needs to understand the purpose and importance of a device before deciding what action to take.

What happens when the information is wrong?

I believe inaccurate or missing asset information is one of the major risks of an autonomous SOC.

Imagine that an important company server is incorrectly listed as an ordinary workstation. If AI detects suspicious activity, it might automatically disconnect the device because it believes that doing so is safe. However, the server might actually be responsible for managing important business activities.

The opposite situation could also happen. An ordinary workstation might be incorrectly marked as a critical server. AI could then avoid disconnecting it because it believes the device is too important. This could allow an attacker to continue for longer.

Possible problems caused by incorrect information:

  • AI may choose the wrong response to a security alert.
  • Important business systems could be disrupted.
  • A real threat might continue because the device is incorrectly classified.
  • Investigations could take longer because important details are missing.
  • Analysts might trust decisions that are based on incorrect information.

For this reason, I do not think AI should make extreme decisions when it cannot confirm the information it needs.

The IBM article also discusses its Automated Threat Operations Machine (ATOM). When information is missing, ATOM can request additional details from other AI agents and security systems. These include vulnerability management, configuration management databases and endpoint detection tools (Velisaris, 2025).

I find this approach useful because the system can try to collect missing information instead of immediately stopping its investigation. However, if the information is still unclear or the recommended action could cause serious damage, a human analyst should review it. Companies should also keep their asset records updated so AI is not making decisions using outdated information.

Is an Autonomous SOC a Good Idea?

Overall, I think implementing autonomous SOC systems could be a good idea, but humans should not be completely removed.

Larger businesses may have more resources, updated systems and advanced security technology. This could make it easier for them to use AI for routine security tasks.

Smaller businesses may struggle more. If their asset information is outdated or they do not have enough staff to supervise AI, allowing it to make decisions without verification could create more problems.

Another important point is that the company is still responsible if something goes wrong because of an automatic AI decision. A responsible SOC should clearly explain what AI is allowed to do. Human analysts should also be able to review, stop or correct decisions when necessary.

The NIST framework explains that AI risks should be managed throughout the entire life cycle of an AI system, rather than only when it is first introduced (Tabassi, 2023).

This means companies should regularly check whether their AI systems are working correctly. They should test automatic responses and make improvements whenever necessary. AI should be viewed as an addition to a security team, not the only protection a business has.

Skills Required to Become a SOC Pilot

In the future, people working in cybersecurity will need to understand how to work with AI. If I want to become a SOC pilot, there are several skills I will need to develop.

01. Cybersecurity Basics

I need to understand networking, operating systems, common cyberattacks and how hackers target computer systems.

02. Threat Detection

I need to improve my investigation skills, understand log files, identify suspicious activity and become familiar with SIEM, EDR and SOAR tools.

03. Critical Thinking

I should not blindly trust AI decisions. I need to understand why an action is recommended and consider what could happen if I follow it.

04. Knowledge of AI

Understanding how AI agents work and what can cause errors in their decisions will help me supervise them more effectively.

05. Communication Skills

I need to explain cybersecurity problems clearly to other people in the organisation, including those without technical knowledge.

06. Continuous Learning

Cybersecurity is constantly changing. I need to keep learning about new threats, tools and technologies to stay prepared.

Rather than trying to be faster than AI, I would like to become knowledgeable about how to use it. This will help me understand its decisions and take control when it cannot handle a situation properly.

Conclusion

Based on the IBM article, I believe SOC pilots could become more common in cybersecurity in the future. AI can help security teams complete repetitive tasks, detect threats and respond to common attacks more quickly.

However, I do not believe it would be responsible to allow AI to make every security decision. It can still make mistakes, especially when it does not have accurate information about the affected device. This could disrupt important services or allow attacks to continue.

I believe tasks that have been tested and found to be safe should be handled by AI. However, when a situation is unfamiliar, important systems are involved or information is uncertain, human analysts should remain involved.

Becoming a SOC pilot will require cybersecurity knowledge, critical thinking, problem-solving and good decision-making skills. Although AI will change the way security analysts work, I do not believe human analysts will become unnecessary.

The important thing is learning how to work with AI without completely depending on it.
